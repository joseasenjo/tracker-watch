Browser check

Every time you open a web page, your browser answers a number of questions about itself. This page asks them the way a tracking script would, shows you the answers and explains what each one is for.

Nothing happens until you press a button. The first button reads your browser inside this page: it makes no request to any server, sets no cookie and stores nothing, not even in your browser. Close the tab and it is gone. The two checks further down are optional and do contact other servers, as each one explains before you press it.

Takes about a second.

Compare with another browser or a private window

Open this page in the other browser (or in a private window), read it there, press "Copy this report as text" and paste the text here. The comparison happens in this page; nothing is sent. It shows which details change and which do not: switching browser changes many, while a private window usually changes few, because the hardware and settings are the same.


Are trackers blocked? An optional test

The reading above looks at your browser. This test looks at your protection: it asks for a well-known script from seven advertising and analytics servers and sees whether your browser lets the request through. It is the only part of this site that contacts other companies, so it never runs by itself.

What exactly is contacted, and what do they learn?

When you press the button, your browser makes seven requests, one to each of these servers: pagead2.googlesyndication.com, securepubads.g.doubleclick.net, static.ads-twitter.com, bat.bing.com, www.google-analytics.com, connect.facebook.net and sb.scorecardresearch.com. Each one receives what any request carries: your IP address and your browser's user agent. We send no cookies and no referring page. The result stays in this page: it is not stored, and we never receive it. One extra request goes to this site itself, to check that your connection works.

A request that never leaves your browser (blocked by an extension, a filter on your network or a browser setting) counts as blocked. A request that gets any answer counts as reached.

What does your connection reveal? Another optional check

Whatever your browser says about itself, every site also sees the address your requests come from, and a page can sometimes learn an extra address through a feature called WebRTC. This check shows both. It never runs by itself.

What exactly is contacted, and what do they learn?

When you press the button, your browser makes two kinds of request, both to Cloudflare, a company that runs a large part of the web's infrastructure: one to its public connection page (www.cloudflare.com/cdn-cgi/trace), which answers with the address it sees you coming from, and a WebRTC exchange with its public server (stun.cloudflare.com), which is how a page can learn addresses. Cloudflare receives what any request carries, such as your IP address and your browser's user agent; we send no cookies and no referring page. The answers are shown only to you: they are not stored and we never receive them.

How to read the report

Each line is one detail, in the form a script receives it. Open a line to see what it is and how it can be used. The label on the right says how much that kind of detail usually tells apart in published studies: it is a general guide to the kind of detail, not a measurement of your browser.

What is browser fingerprinting?

A cookie is a label a site puts on your browser to recognise it later. You can see and delete cookies, and blocking tools go after them first. A fingerprint is different: nothing is stored on your computer. A script reads many small details that are already there (your screen, fonts, graphics hardware, language, time zone) and combines them. The combination is often different enough from other people's to recognise the same browser again, on the same site or on another one.

That is why deleting cookies does not help against it, and why it matters to know which details your browser gives away.

Why there is no “unique among X browsers” number here

Other tools tell you how rare your combination is, in “bits of information”. A bit is the smallest unit of information: one yes-or-no answer. Every detail that splits the crowd in two adds about one bit, so around 33 bits are enough to single out one person among all of humanity. To say how many bits your details are worth, a tool must have recorded the details of a very large number of other visitors. This site records nothing about anyone, so it has no such population and will not invent a figure.

If you want that comparison, tools run by organisations that collect it for research exist, for example Cover Your Tracks from the Electronic Frontier Foundation, which this page is inspired by. Their sample comes from people who chose to run the test, so read the figure as a guide, not a verdict.

What this page cannot tell you

  • Whether anyone is tracking you. A detail being readable does not mean a site reads it, and a unique combination does not mean you are being followed. It shows what is possible.
  • Whether every tracker is blocked. The optional test asks seven well-known servers. Your protection may treat others differently, so a clean result is a sample, not a guarantee.
  • What your browser would do on other sites. Your extensions and settings can change by site.
